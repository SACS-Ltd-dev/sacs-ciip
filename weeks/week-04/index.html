The OSINT investigative lifecycle

In Week 1 you saw the general intelligence cycle. OSINT work follows the same shape, but it is worth naming each stage in its OSINT specific form, because you will move through these exact stages, consciously, on every single case from here on.

StageWhat it means in OSINT terms
DefineTurn a vague worry into a specific, answerable research question. Not "is this vendor suspicious" but "does the domain, registration, and online footprint of Crestfield Industrial Supplies match a legitimate, established company."
Identify sourcesDecide where the answer is likely to live. WHOIS records, business registries, social media, cached pages, document metadata, technical infrastructure.
CollectSystematically gather from each source, in an order that makes sense, logging everything as you go using the case notes habit from Week 3.
VerifyCross check every finding against at least one other independent source before you trust it. A single source is a lead, not a fact.
AnalyseConnect the verified findings into a picture. Does the evidence support the research question, contradict it, or leave it genuinely open.
ReportWrite it up clearly enough that someone who was not in the room with you can understand what you found and how confident you are in it.

Tab 6 is where you put all of it together into an actual research plan.

Why the order of these stages matters

Beginners often collapse the OSINT lifecycle into a single step in their heads: search until you find something, then stop. Professionals resist that collapse deliberately. Collecting before you have defined your question wastes time chasing details that never mattered. Analysing before you have verified means building conclusions on a foundation that might not hold. Reporting before you have properly analysed means handing someone your raw impressions dressed up as findings.

The lifecycle exists because skipping a stage does not save time, it just moves the cost of that stage to later, usually at a point where it is more expensive to fix. A research question you never properly defined tends to surface again halfway through your report, when you realise you are not actually sure what question your evidence is supposed to be answering.

None of these are separate skills you learn once and forget. They are habits you will keep building for the rest of the program.

Sharpening the collect stage

Google dorking, search operators that actually dig

A search operator narrows a search engine down to exactly what you mean instead of what it guesses you mean. Learn these well and a normal search engine starts behaving like a purpose built investigative tool.

OperatorWhat it doesExample
site:Restricts results to one domainsite:crestfield-industrial.com
filetype:Restricts results to a file typefiletype:pdf
"exact phrase"Matches the exact wording, not a paraphrase"Crestfield Industrial Supplies"
-excludeRemoves a term from results entirely-site:crestfield-industrial.com
ORMatches either term, useful for spelling variantssite:a.com OR site:b.com
intitle:Word must appear in the page titleintitle:login
inurl:Word must appear in the page addressinurl:admin
cache:Shows the search engine's last saved copy of a pagecache:example.com

Why operators work the way they do

Search engines build an index, a giant catalogue of what is on each page they have crawled, roughly analogous to the index at the back of a book. A plain keyword search asks the index "which pages mention this word." An operator asks a much narrower, structured question instead, "which pages at this specific domain, of this specific file type, mention this exact phrase." That structure is exactly why operators return sharper, smaller, more usable result sets than a plain search ever could, you are no longer asking the index a vague question and hoping the ranking algorithm guesses your intent correctly.

Two more operators worth knowing beyond the core set: allintitle: requires every word after it to appear in the page title, useful for finding pages built specifically around a topic rather than pages that mention it in passing, and related: finds sites similar to a domain you already know, occasionally surfacing a competitor or a linked shell company you did not know to search for directly.

Searching the internet's infrastructure

Shodan and Censys, search engines for devices

Google indexes web pages. Shodan and Censys index devices, servers, cameras, industrial equipment, anything connected to the internet that answers when it is asked a question. They are free to use at a basic level and completely legal, because they only surface information that was already publicly broadcasting itself.

The authorisation rule still applies. Browsing what Shodan and Censys have already indexed is legal, you are reading a public library, not breaking into anything. Actively scanning a target yourself with a tool like Nmap is different and needs explicit authorisation. For this week's hands on lab, your authorised target is scanme.nmap.org, a host the Nmap project itself set up and explicitly permits people to practise scanning against. Never point a scanner at anything else without clear written authorisation.

Why this matters for an investigation

A device left exposed to the internet without protection is one of the most common entry points behind real incidents, an unprotected database, a default password left unchanged on a camera, a remote desktop port open to anyone. When you are investigating an organisation's security posture, or trying to understand how a fraud ring's infrastructure is put together, this is often where the story starts.

The scale of this problem is well documented. The Mirai botnet, one of the most disruptive cyberattacks in recent history, was built almost entirely out of ordinary internet connected cameras and routers left with their factory default passwords, devices that anyone running a basic Shodan search could have found sitting exposed months before the attack. That single pattern, easily searchable, widely ignored, took down major internet services across the United States in 2016. It remains one of the clearest illustrations of why a tool as simple as a search engine for devices carries real investigative weight.

The source hiding inside every document

FOCA and document metadata

Every document carries information about itself that most people never look at, who created it, what software they used, sometimes even an internal file path from the computer it was written on. FOCA is a free Windows tool built specifically to harvest this from every document it can find across a target domain, all at once.

You do not need FOCA installed to start this exercise. The same metadata is visible through simpler means, right click a file's Properties on Windows, use Get Info on macOS, or use a free cross platform tool like ExifTool. What matters is the habit of checking, not the specific tool.

Why this data exists in the first place

Metadata is not a mistake or a flaw, it is a normal, intended feature of how modern office software works. Word processors record an author name to support version tracking and collaboration. Templates carry file paths so the software can find related resources. None of this is designed with an outside investigator in mind, which is exactly why it so often survives untouched, the people creating these documents are focused on the visible content, not on what the file quietly carries underneath it.

Organisations that handle sensitive material professionally often run a "document sanitisation" step before publishing anything externally, stripping exactly this kind of metadata deliberately. Its absence is itself informative: a company that habitually leaks this data across its published documents likely has no such process in place at all, which can be a small but genuine signal about how seriously an organisation takes information security more broadly.

Why this is a real technique, not a novelty. Metadata harvesting like this has repeatedly exposed internal usernames, software versions, and network layouts of organisations who never intended to share any of it. It is one of the quietest, most reliable OSINT techniques that exists, because almost nobody thinks to scrub it before sharing a file.
Seeing the connections, not just the facts

A first look at Maltego

Every technique so far in this program produces individual facts, a domain, a name, an IP address, a metadata field. Maltego's entire purpose is turning a pile of individual facts into a visual map of how they connect.

Maltego works with entities, things like a domain, a person, an email address, a phone number, and transforms, automated queries that take one entity and find others connected to it. Feed it a domain, run a transform, and it can pull back the IP address, the registrant email, other domains sharing that email, and lay all of it out as a connected graph in front of you.

The value of this over doing the same lookups manually is not speed alone, though a machine running dozens of queries in seconds is certainly faster than typing each one by hand. The real value is that a graph makes patterns visible that a list of separate findings hides. Ten domains sharing one registrant email look like ten unrelated facts in a spreadsheet. The same ten domains in a Maltego graph look like a single hub with ten spokes, and that shape is immediately, visually obvious in a way rows of text rarely are.

What separates a strong research plan from a weak one

A weak research plan is a list of tools someone intends to try. A strong one reads almost like a hypothesis test: it states plainly what would count as evidence for the concern, what would count as evidence against it, and in what order the investigator plans to check each source so that time is not wasted chasing a low value lookup before a high value one. Mentors reading these plans are looking specifically for that structure, not just a list of tool names copied from Tab 2 through 5.

It is also worth stating your threshold for action explicitly, before you have any findings, rather than deciding after the fact what counts as suspicious enough. Deciding your threshold in advance is a small discipline that protects you from a very human bias, unconsciously lowering the bar for "suspicious enough" once you have already invested time looking for something.
