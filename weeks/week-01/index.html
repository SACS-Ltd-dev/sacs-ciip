Welcome to the work

Before you touch a single tool, you need to understand the shape of this field: what it actually is, who does it, and why it matters here, in Nigeria, right now.

Here is the plain truth about this program. You are not here to become a hacker. You are here to become someone who can find people, follow money, read digital evidence, and explain what happened, clearly enough that a court, a company, or a victim can act on it.

That is a different skill from breaking into systems. It is closer to what a detective, an auditor, and a journalist do, except the evidence lives on phones, in databases, on blockchains, and sometimes in places that take real courage and discipline to visit safely. Over the next six months, you will build all four of those muscles: finding information, preserving evidence, understanding the people behind the crime, and following the money.

Nigeria is not a footnote in this story. It sits at the centre of it. Nigerian investigators, working with the EFCC, the police cybercrime units, and international partners, have helped take down some of the most well documented online fraud operations in the world, cases you will study in the next tab. Nigerian fraud rings have also, unfortunately, been behind some of the most damaging business email compromise schemes on record. Both of those facts are why this training exists. The same skills that catch a fraud ring are the skills a fraud ring's victims wish someone had used sooner.

A note on tone. Some of what you learn here is heavy. You will read about real victims, real prison sentences, and real psychological manipulation. Treat every case with the seriousness you would want if it were your own business or your own family on the other end of that email.

The four roles inside this field

Most people imagine a single generic "cybersecurity investigator." In reality, the work splits into four distinct roles that often sit on the same case at the same time. Click each card to see what a normal week looks like for that person.

OSINT Analyst

O
Finds and connects information that is already public, if you know where and how to look.

An OSINT analyst starts with almost nothing, maybe a name, a phone number, or a username, and builds a picture: where this person works, who they know, what platforms they use, where they have been seen online. None of this requires breaking into anything. It requires knowing which search engines, registries, and social platforms hold the pieces, and how to verify that what you found is actually true and not a coincidence of a shared name.

A realistic week: tracing a fraud suspect's Instagram handle across five platforms, confirming a company's ownership through public registry records, geolocating a photo a scammer posted by reading the shadows and signage in the background.

Typical tools: Google and Bing search operators, Maltego, Shodan, Sherlock, WHOIS lookups.

DFIR Examiner

D
Recovers and preserves digital evidence from devices, in a way that holds up in court.

DFIR stands for Digital Forensics and Incident Response. This person is handed a laptop, a phone, or a hard drive and has to answer: what happened on this device, when, and who did it. Every step has to be documented well enough that a defence lawyer cannot later claim the evidence was tampered with.

A realistic week: imaging a seized laptop with a write blocker so nothing on the original disk changes, recovering deleted chat logs, building a timeline of when a suspicious file was created, opened, and emailed out.

Typical tools: Autopsy, FTK Imager, Volatility, Wireshark.

CTI Analyst

C
Studies how attackers operate so organisations can recognise and stop them earlier next time.

Cyber Threat Intelligence is less about one case and more about patterns. A CTI analyst asks: who is behind this style of attack, what have they done before, what will they likely do next. They turn scattered technical clues into a profile that a security team can actually act on.

A realistic week: mapping a phishing campaign's infrastructure back to a known fraud group, writing a briefing that tells a bank's security team what to watch for this month, tracking a ransomware group's leak site for new victims.

Typical tools: MITRE ATT&CK, MISP, threat feeds, link analysis software.

Cybercrime Investigator

I
Holds the whole case together, evidence, law, and people, and drives it toward a result.

This is often the coordinating role. A cybercrime investigator understands enough OSINT, forensics, and threat intelligence to direct all three, while also understanding what a prosecutor needs to actually charge someone. They interview victims and witnesses, decide what evidence to request, and write the report that everything else feeds into.

A realistic week: taking a fraud report from a small business owner, deciding which leads to chase first, coordinating with a forensic examiner on a seized phone, and preparing a case summary for referral to the EFCC.

Typical tools: case management systems, all of the above, plus a great deal of interviewing skill.

The intelligence cycle: how a case actually moves

Every investigation, no matter how small, moves through the same six stages. Click through them using one running example: a small Lagos business owner reports that her supplier's bank account details "changed" and she just wired 4 million naira to a fraudster.

STEP 1

Direction

Someone defines the question. Here: who received the 4 million naira, and can any of it still be recovered or traced before it moves further?
STEP 2

Collection

Gather everything available: the fraudulent email, the new bank account number, the business owner's original invoice, any WhatsApp messages exchanged before the transfer.
STEP 3

Processing

Turn raw material into something usable. Extract the sender's real email headers, pull the destination bank account's registered name, timestamp every message in order.
STEP 4

Analysis

Make sense of it. The email header shows the message came from a look alike domain, not the real supplier. The receiving account was opened three weeks ago and has moved similar sums six times this month, a pattern of a mule account.
STEP 5

Dissemination

Share the findings with the people who can act. A report goes to the bank's fraud desk requesting an account freeze, and a case file goes to the EFCC with everything organised and dated.
STEP 6

Feedback

Learn from the outcome. Was the account frozen in time? What would have caught this faster? That lesson feeds directly back into Step 1 of the next case.
Where this goes next. Every phase of this program builds one part of this cycle. Phase 1 sharpens Collection through OSINT. Phase 2 sharpens Processing and evidence handling through forensics. Phase 6 sharpens Analysis through structured threat intelligence frameworks. By the capstone, you will run the full cycle yourself, start to finish.
Real investigations, real outcomes

Two Nigerian cases every investigator should know

These are not hypothetical scenarios. Both men were real, both cases were prosecuted in United States federal courts, and both show exactly why this program teaches OSINT, forensics, financial tracing, and law together instead of as separate subjects.

CLOSED · SENTENCED

The Hushpuppi Case

Ramon Olorunwa Abbas, also known as "Ray Hushpuppi"

Ramon Abbas built a public image as a wealthy Dubai based businessman, posting photographs of private jets, luxury cars, and designer clothing to millions of Instagram followers. By his own account, he began as what is locally called a "Yahoo boy," a term for young Nigerians who run online fraud schemes. His operation grew far beyond simple scams into large scale money laundering for business email compromise fraud and cyber heists carried out by other criminal groups, reportedly including a scheme connected to North Korean state linked hackers who stole tens of millions of dollars from a bank in Malta and moved it through banks in Romania and Bulgaria.

He was arrested in Dubai in June 2020 following an FBI operation, extradited to the United States, and pleaded guilty in 2021 to conspiracy to commit money laundering. In November 2022, a federal judge in Los Angeles sentenced him to 135 months, just over 11 years, and ordered him to pay more than 1.7 million dollars in restitution to two of his victims. Prosecutors said that during just an 18 month window, he had conspired to launder over 300 million dollars.

The Invictus Obi Case

Obinwanne Okeke, also known as "Invictus Obi"

Before his arrest, Obinwanne Okeke was one of the most celebrated young entrepreneurs in Nigeria, featured on the cover of Forbes Africa and named to its 30 Under 30 list. He ran a group of companies called Invictus Group, with claimed interests spanning oil and gas, agriculture, and technology. Underneath that public image, prosecutors found a business email compromise operation running from around 2015 to 2019 that caused approximately 11 million dollars in losses.

The most damaging single scheme targeted Unatrac Holding Limited, the export sales office for the heavy equipment company Caterpillar. In April 2018, one Unatrac executive opened a phishing email that looked legitimate and entered their login credentials into a fake page. That one moment gave the fraud ring access to the executive's real email account. From inside that account, the conspirators sent fraudulent wire transfer instructions and fake invoices to Unatrac's finance team, redirecting nearly 11 million dollars to accounts they controlled.

Okeke was arrested by the FBI at Dulles Airport in Virginia in August 2019, just before boarding a flight back to Nigeria after visiting the United States for the birth of his daughter. He pleaded guilty in 2020 and was sentenced in February 2021 to 10 years in federal prison.

Why investigators study this case. This case is a masterclass in how small a crack has to be. One phishing email, one distracted click, and a company lost 11 million dollars. It also teaches a harder lesson about assumptions: Okeke had fame, legitimate businesses, and public praise. A good investigator follows the evidence, not the reputation. Never let someone's public image talk you out of following a lead.
What both cases have in common. Nigerian nationals, business email compromise as the core method, an eventual arrest built on financial trails and digital evidence, and public social media activity or business dealings that gave investigators an opening. This is exactly the combination of OSINT, forensics, financial tracing, and legal process this program is built around.

Try it yourself: spot the red flags

Below is a fictionalised business email compromise message, built in the style of the Unatrac case. Click on anything that looks suspicious to see why it matters. Find all five flags.

INBOX > Finance Team > 1 unread message
From: accounts@untarac-holdings.com
This domain reads almost like the real one, but the letters are swapped: "untarac" instead of "unatrac." This is called a lookalike or typosquatted domain, and it is one of the most common tricks in business email compromise. Always check the exact spelling of a sender's domain, letter by letter.
To: finance@yourcompany.com
Subject: URGENT: Updated payment details, action needed before close of business today
Urgency and deadline pressure is deliberate. Fraudsters want you moving too fast to stop and verify. A genuine finance department rarely demands an irreversible wire transfer within hours.
Hello team,

Please note that our bank has changed our account details due to an ongoing audit. Kindly update your records to the new account below and process the outstanding invoice today, as any delay will affect our delivery schedule.

New Account Name: Unatrac Holdings Ltd
New Account Number: 0041-778-2291, GreenTrust MFB

Please do not call our office to confirm as our phone lines are affected by the audit. Reply to this email only.

Regards,
Finance Department
This sentence does the real work of the scam, it instructs the reader to act on the new details embedded further down, wrapped in ordinary sounding business language. The instruction to act is buried inside a routine looking paragraph so it does not stand out on a first read.
This is the actual fraudulent change, the one detail that redirects real money to the fraudster's account. Everything else in the email exists purely to make this one line look ordinary and get it actioned quickly.
This line tries to cut off the exact safety check that would catch the fraud, calling a number you already had on file. Any message that discourages you from verifying through a channel you already trust should be treated as a serious warning sign on its own.

The rule this teaches. A changed bank account for an existing supplier is one of the single biggest red flags in fraud investigation. The correct response is always to verify the change through a phone number you already had on file before the email arrived, never a number or instruction inside the email itself.
The framework that protects you and your work

Law and ethics, in plain language

You cannot do this work well without understanding what the law actually permits, what it forbids, and where the ethical line sits even when the law is silent. Click each section open.

The Cybercrimes Act, what it actually covers▶

The Cybercrimes (Prohibition, Prevention, etc.) Act 2015, as amended, is Nigeria's main law governing computer and internet based crime. You do not need to memorise it section by section, but you do need to know what it makes illegal, because those are the exact behaviours you will be investigating, and the exact behaviours you must never commit yourself, even accidentally, while practising.

  • Unauthorised access to a computer system or network, sometimes called hacking, whether or not any damage is caused.
  • Cyberstalking and cyberbullying, including repeated unwanted digital contact intended to harass or intimidate.
  • Identity theft and impersonation carried out through electronic means.
  • Electronic fraud, which covers the business email compromise pattern you saw in Tab 2.
  • Child sexual abuse material offences, treated with the highest severity in the Act.
  • Cyberterrorism and critical infrastructure attacks.

The law also sets out how evidence must be gathered and preserved for it to be admissible, including the need for proper court orders before certain kinds of access, and it names the bodies responsible for enforcement, principally the EFCC, the Nigeria Police Force, and NITDA in a supporting regulatory role.

The Nigeria Data Protection Act, what it means for you as an investigator▶

The NDPA 2023 governs how personal data, meaning any information that identifies a living person, must be collected, stored, and used. As someone learning OSINT and forensics, you will be handling personal data constantly, even in practice exercises. Three things matter most for you right now.

  • You need a lawful basis to process someone's personal data. In a legitimate investigation, that basis usually exists. In casual curiosity about someone you know, it usually does not.
  • Data must be collected for a specific purpose and not reused carelessly for something else. Information you gather for one case file does not become fair game for unrelated personal use.
  • People have rights over their own data, including the right to know it exists and, in many cases, the right to have it corrected or deleted. This is part of why SACS case data is never real, unconsented personal information, and why you will never practise on a real person without their explicit permission.
Chain of custody, the idea you will hear constantly▶

Chain of custody means being able to prove, step by step, exactly who touched a piece of evidence, when, and what they did to it, from the moment it was collected to the moment it is presented in court. If there is a gap in that chain, a defence lawyer can argue the evidence may have been altered, and the whole case can collapse, no matter how strong the evidence looked.

You will build full chain of custody skills in Phase 2. For now, just remember the principle: in this field, how you found something matters almost as much as what you found.

Four ethical dilemmas, what would you actually do

Read each scenario and choose an answer. There is a clearly correct choice in every case, chosen to match the Code of Conduct you will sign this week.

Scenario 1. While practising OSINT techniques for a class exercise, you accidentally find what looks like the real, current address of your ex partner. What do you do?
Save the address. It might be useful information to have someday.
Close it out immediately and do not act on it. This is outside the scope of your authorised practice.
Share it with a friend who has also had issues with this person.
Scenario 2. A friend outside the program asks you to "just check where this guy works" because they suspect he is a scammer. What do you do?
Help them out quickly, it seems harmless and you have the skills now.
Do it, but only using tools from the program lab so it does not count as "real world."
Decline, and if they have a genuine concern, point them toward reporting it through a proper channel like the bank or the EFCC.
Scenario 3. During a supervised dark web exercise, you land on a page that appears to contain child sexual abuse material. What is the very first thing you do?
Close the page immediately without saving, screenshotting, or scrolling further, then report it to your mentor the same day.
Take a screenshot first as proof, then close it and report.
Note the URL by keeping the tab open in the background so you do not lose it before reporting.
Scenario 4. You are building a case file for a mock investigation and realise one of the "practice" data points actually matches a real, identifiable person. What do you do?
Keep working, it is probably just a coincidence of names.
Stop and report it to your mentor right away so the material can be reviewed and corrected.
Quietly change the name in your own notes and continue.

Week 1 glossary

OSINT +
Open Source Intelligence. Information gathered from publicly available sources, no hacking or unauthorised access involved.
DFIR +
Digital Forensics and Incident Response. Recovering and analysing evidence directly from devices and systems.
CTI +
Cyber Threat Intelligence. Studying attacker patterns and infrastructure to predict and prevent future incidents.
BEC +
Business Email Compromise. Fraud carried out by impersonating a trusted business contact through email, usually to redirect a payment.
Chain of custody +
A documented, unbroken record of who handled a piece of evidence, when, and what they did to it.
NDPA +
Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023. Governs how personal data must be collected, used, and protected.
