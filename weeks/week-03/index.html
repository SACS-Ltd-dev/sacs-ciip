Building your investigator persona, staying compartmentalised, sound case notes, and protecting your own wellbeing

Foundations, final week

The week that protects everything else

You now understand the roles, the landscape, and the plumbing of the internet. This week is different. It is about protecting yourself, your evidence, and your own mind while you do this work, for the rest of your career, not just this program.

Here is something worth sitting with. An investigator who gets identified by the people they are investigating is not just personally at risk. Their case can collapse, their organisation can be compromised, and future investigations by anyone connected to them become harder. Discipline here is not paranoia. It is professional hygiene, the same way a surgeon scrubs in before an operation, not because every patient is dangerous, but because the cost of skipping the habit even once can be severe.

This week covers four things that fit together as one system: building a research identity that cannot be traced back to you, using a VPN correctly and understanding its real limits, building a case notes habit that will serve you for years, and recognising the early signs of burnout before this work starts costing you more than it should.

Why this comes before the technical phases. Starting in Phase 1, you begin real OSINT practice. Starting in Phase 5, you will access the dark web. Both require the habits in this week already being second nature. We are building the seatbelt before you learn to drive fast.

A pattern worth knowing before you start

Open source investigators, journalists, and researchers who work publicly have, on more than one documented occasion, found themselves targeted in return once their work drew enough attention. Bellingcat, the well known open source investigation collective, has spoken publicly about members being named, targeted, and harassed by hostile actors after their research became prominent, including during their work on state linked incidents. None of this happened because the researchers did anything wrong technically. It happened because their identities became linked to their work, sometimes through nothing more than a reused photo or a consistent writing style across a real and a research account.

You are not likely to attract that level of attention in a training exercise. But the pattern holds at every scale. A local fraud ring you investigate professionally one day may have far more capacity and willingness to retaliate than a stranger would guess, and the habits that protect a globally known investigator are exactly the same habits that protect you on your very first real case. Build them now, while the stakes are still training exercises, so they are automatic by the time the stakes are real.

The four things this week actually builds

Everything in this week's four remaining tabs supports one single goal: making sure that nothing about how you conduct an investigation can be turned around and used against you, your evidence, or the people you are trying to help. Concretely, that means:

  • A research identity that cannot be traced back to your real one, even under scrutiny (Tab 2).
  • Network level protection that hides your real location from the people you investigate (Tab 3).
  • A documentation habit that makes your findings defensible months or years later, not just convincing to you today (Tab 4).
  • Enough self awareness to notice when this work is costing you more than it should, before it becomes a real problem (Tab 6).

None of these are separate skills you learn once and forget. They are habits you will keep building for the rest of the program, and realistically, for the rest of your career in this field.

Cover that actually holds up

Building your investigation persona

A sock puppet is a research identity, a separate email, username, and sometimes social media presence, that is not connected back to your real one. Used properly, within the boundaries of your Ethics Agreement, it lets you observe and research without alerting the subject or exposing yourself.

Read this before anything else in this tab. A sock puppet is a research tool, not a licence to deceive, harass, or manipulate anyone into sharing information they would not otherwise share. It exists purely so your presence does not tip off a subject or expose your real identity. Everything you learned in Week 1's ethics tab still applies in full.

Why a persona needs to be more than an alias

New investigators sometimes treat a sock puppet as nothing more than a fake name attached to their real thinking. That is precisely the mistake that gets personas unmasked. A subject, or an automated platform detection system, is not looking for a name, they are looking for a pattern. Writing style, the specific hours an account is active, which other accounts it interacts with, how quickly it replies, all of these form a fingerprint that exists whether or not you intended it to.

This is why professional analysts treat a persona less like a costume and more like a character with its own consistent, boring, believable life. A persona that has "always" worked in a mid level admin role, lives in a specific but unremarkable part of a city, and posts about ordinary things at ordinary times is far harder to unravel than a persona that exists purely to approach one subject and otherwise shows no signs of life at all.

Below is a fictional research profile a mentee built for a class exercise. It has five serious OPSEC problems. Click on anything that looks like a mistake.

👤
Amara Johnson
@amara_johnson_ng · Joined 3 days ago
A brand new account with no history looks exactly like what it is. Anyone who checks your puppet's join date and finds nothing behind it will immediately suspect it is fake. A working persona needs weeks of quiet, believable activity before it is ever used to approach a real subject.
Lagos based. Marketing enthusiast. Same profile photo I use on my personal Instagram, just cropped differently
A reverse image search takes seconds and instantly connects this "separate" identity straight back to the investigator's real account. Never reuse a real photo of yourself, a colleague, or anyone identifiable, even cropped or filtered.
Location: Lives in the same estate I actually live in, mentioned in three posts
Specific, real, personal location details create a direct line back to the investigator's actual life. A persona's background details should be plausible and consistent, but never true facts borrowed from your own life.
Recovery: Recovery phone number is my personal number
If this account is ever compromised, reported, or investigated in return, the recovery phone number is often the very first thing that gets pulled. It should always be a separate number, ideally one not tied to your name at all.
Activity: Always active between 9pm and midnight, matching my real sleep schedule exactly
Consistent activity patterns are a known deanonymisation technique. An analyst comparing your puppet's active hours against your real account's active hours, if anyone ever suspects a connection, can find a strong statistical match. Vary your persona's activity pattern deliberately.

Building it properly

DO

  • Use a dedicated email created purely for this purpose, never your personal or work address
  • Use a stock or AI generated face, or no photo at all, never a real identifiable person
  • Keep a private written log of every persona detail so you stay consistent over time
  • Let the persona build a quiet, believable history before you ever use it actively
  • Keep personas and your real accounts open in separate browser profiles or containers

DO NOT

  • Reuse any password, phone number, or recovery email from your real accounts
  • Use the persona to harass, threaten, or manipulate anyone into unsafe disclosure
  • Log into a personal account from the same browser session as an active persona
  • Give the persona a real address, workplace, or biographical detail that is actually yours
  • Leave a persona logged in indefinitely without a clear investigative reason tied to a case
One browser habit that solves half of this. Modern browsers let you create separate profiles, or in Firefox's case, Multi Account Containers, that keep cookies, logins, and history completely walled off from each other. Set up one container for your real identity and a separate one for each active persona. This single habit prevents the most common accidental exposure: a platform quietly linking two accounts because they were open in the same browser session.
What a VPN actually does

VPNs, and their honest limits

A VPN, virtual private network, routes your internet traffic through a separate server before it reaches its destination. Toggle the switch below to see what that actually changes.

With the VPN off, the website you visit sees your real IP address directly. Anyone with legal access to that website's logs, or the website owner themselves if they are the very fraud ring you are investigating, can potentially trace that address back toward you.
What a VPN does not do. It does not make you anonymous. It does not stop a website from fingerprinting your browser through other means, screen size, fonts, installed extensions. It does not protect you if you log into a personal account while connected. It does not encrypt anything once your traffic leaves the VPN server on its way to the destination. Treat it as one layer of a system, never as a magic cloak.

What is actually happening underneath

When you connect to a VPN, your device builds an encrypted tunnel to the VPN provider's server before any of your traffic touches the wider internet. Everything inside that tunnel, including which websites you visit, is unreadable to anyone watching your local network or your internet provider's equipment. Once your traffic reaches the VPN server, though, it has to leave the tunnel to actually reach the website you wanted, at which point it looks, to that website, exactly like it came from the VPN server's location, not yours.

This is precisely why a VPN protects you from your internet provider or a local network snooping on your destination, but does nothing once the connection reaches its actual endpoint. If the website itself is compromised, or run by the very people you are investigating, a VPN only hides where you connected from, not what you did once you got there. That distinction trips up more beginners than any other part of this topic.

A failure mode worth knowing: the DNS leak

Even with a VPN connected, some poorly configured setups still send DNS lookups, the requests that translate a website name into an address, outside the encrypted tunnel, straight to your regular internet provider. This is called a DNS leak, and it can reveal exactly which sites you visited even while your VPN otherwise looks like it is working correctly. Reputable VPN providers build in DNS leak protection by default, but it is worth checking your setup at a free site like dnsleaktest.com the first time you configure a new VPN, and periodically afterward.

Compartmentalisation, the bigger habit VPNs sit inside

A VPN is one piece of a larger discipline called compartmentalisation, keeping every part of your investigation life separated from every part of your personal life so that a mistake in one place cannot expose the other. A simple mental checklist before opening any investigation work:

Before you start a session

  • VPN connected and confirmed active
  • Correct browser profile or container open, not your personal one
  • Persona credentials, not personal ones, ready if needed
  • Case notes file open and ready to log as you go

Case notes, and a couple of quick tools

Months from now, in a real case, someone may ask you exactly how you found something, and exactly when. If your notes cannot answer that, the finding may be far less useful, even if it was completely correct.

From theory to real decisions

The intelligence cycle, operationally

In Week 1 you saw the six stages of the intelligence cycle laid out. Now you will actually make decisions at each stage, in a realistic scenario, and see how your choices affect the strength of the case.

The scenario. A small business owner in Abuja reports that she has been chatting for two months with a man she met online, who says he is an American engineer working on an oil rig. He has now asked her to send 800,000 naira to cover an emergency medical bill, promising to pay her back the moment he returns. She is unsure and has come to you before sending anything.
Stage 1, Direction
What is the single most useful question to define before you start gathering anything?
Is this man definitely a criminal
Is there enough evidence here to advise her not to send the money, and separately, can this profile be linked to other known scam activity
What is his real name
Stage 2, Collection
Which piece of evidence should you prioritise collecting first from her?
Her bank account password, so you can check it yourself
His home address, which she probably does not have anyway
Screenshots of the full conversation, his profile photos, and any other names or numbers he has given her
Stage 3, Processing
You now have his profile photos. What is the correct next processing step?
Run a reverse image search on the photos to check whether they belong to someone else entirely
Message him yourself pretending to be her to ask more questions
Report the account immediately without checking anything first
Stage 4, Analysis
The reverse image search shows his photos actually belong to a completely different real person, a photographer with no connection to oil rigs at all. What does this tell you, analytically?
Nothing conclusive, photos get mixed up online all the time
This is a very strong indicator of a romance scam, stolen identity photos are one of the most consistent patterns in this fraud type
It proves he is definitely a specific named criminal
Stage 5, Dissemination
How should you deliver this finding to her?
Simply tell her "he is fake" with no explanation and move on
Post the evidence publicly to warn others before telling her directly
Sit with her, show her the stolen photo match clearly, explain how romance scams typically work, and support her decision on what to do next, including reporting the account
Stage 6, Feedback
A week later, she tells you the same profile tried to contact two of her friends too. What is the correct feedback step?
Fold this new information back into your case notes, it may indicate a wider operation worth flagging to the platform or reporting further
Ignore it, your case with her is already closed
Contact the friends yourself directly without her involvement
The part of the job people skip talking about

Staying well while doing this work

Investigators, journalists, and moderators who regularly encounter fraud, exploitation, and abuse material can experience something called vicarious trauma, real psychological strain that builds from repeated exposure to other people's suffering, even when nothing happened to you directly.

This is not a sign of weakness and it is not rare. It is a documented occupational reality in this field, studied seriously by organisations like the Dart Center for Journalism and Trauma, which publishes free, practical guidance for exactly this kind of work. Naming it clearly now, in Week 3, means you will recognise it in yourself later instead of pushing through silently.

The mechanism behind vicarious trauma is worth understanding, not just the label. Repeated exposure to accounts of harm, fraud that ruined a real family, manipulation that targeted someone vulnerable, can gradually shift how safe and trusting the world feels to the person doing the observing, even though nothing physically happened to them. This is a well documented pattern among journalists, therapists, emergency dispatchers, and content moderators, and there is no reason to expect investigators are somehow immune to it. The people who do this work well over a long career are not the ones who never feel it. They are the ones who built habits to process it before it accumulated.

Signs worth paying attention to in yourself. Trouble sleeping after a heavy case, feeling emotionally numb or unusually irritable, dreading opening your case files, intrusive thoughts about something you encountered, or a creeping sense of cynicism about people in general. Any one of these occasionally is normal. A pattern building over weeks is worth acting on.

Habits that genuinely help

Build these in from week one

  • Hard stop times, this work will always have one more lead, you decide when the day ends
  • A debrief habit, even five minutes telling a mentor or peer what a hard case involved
  • Physical movement after heavy sessions, a short walk resets your nervous system more than scrolling does
  • Rotating heavy and light tasks within the same week rather than stacking difficult cases back to back

Avoid these patterns

  • Treating every wave of dark feelings as something to just push through alone
  • Comparing your reaction to a colleague's and deciding yours is "too sensitive"
  • Numbing with distraction instead of actually processing what you saw
  • Waiting until burnout is severe before telling anyone

A private self check

This stays in your own browser. Nobody else sees it. Be honest with yourself.

I have felt noticeably more irritable or numb after a recent training session
I have had trouble sleeping after reading about a hard case
I have caught myself thinking about a case outside of study hours, unable to switch off
I have not told anyone when something in this training genuinely unsettled me
What to do with this. If you checked two or more boxes, that is worth a conversation with your mentor, not a red flag against you. Mentors expect this conversation and it will never count against your standing in the program. If you are ever in genuine crisis, please reach out to a mental health professional or a crisis line in your area without delay.
