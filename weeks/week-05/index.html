About the practice subject. Tunde Bakare and every social profile you will see in this resource are entirely fictional, built by SACS purely for training. You are not researching a real person. The techniques, however, are exactly what you would use on a real, authorised case.

What SOCMINT actually is

Social Media Intelligence, SOCMINT, is the practice of gathering and analysing publicly available information from social platforms to answer a specific investigative question. It is a subset of OSINT, narrowed to the platforms where people voluntarily reveal the most about their real lives, who they know, where they go, what they believe, and increasingly, what they are trying to hide.

Remember Ramon Abbas from Week 1. His own Instagram activity, posted voluntarily to impress an audience, became some of the strongest evidence against him. That is SOCMINT working exactly as intended, patient, methodical, entirely open source.

The habit underneath every technique this week

SOCMINT rewards patience far more than cleverness. A single Instagram photo rarely proves anything on its own. What actually builds a case is the accumulation of small, individually weak signals that, taken together, stop looking like coincidence. An account created recently is weak on its own, most real people join new platforms too. The same account using a stock photo is still weak alone, plenty of privacy conscious people do that deliberately. But a recently created account, using a stock photo, with almost no connections, claiming a senior role it has no history for, stops looking like any one of those things and starts looking like a pattern.

This is the single most important discipline to bring into Tab 6. Do not stop at the first suspicious detail and declare the case solved. Check every platform you have access to, log every finding regardless of how minor it seems in isolation, and let the pattern across all of them do the actual persuading.

Before you touch a single platform

Platform rules and legal limits

Everything technically possible in SOCMINT is not automatically permitted. This tab sets the boundary before Tab 3 hands you the technique.

Public does not mean unlimited▶

A person posting publicly has accepted that other people, including investigators, may view what they posted. They have not automatically consented to it being scraped in bulk, stored indefinitely, republished, or used to harass them. Manual, individual browsing of public content for a specific investigative purpose sits in very different territory from automated mass collection.

Terms of service still matter▶

Almost every major platform's terms of service prohibit automated scraping, creating fake accounts to deceive users, or using the platform to harass someone. Viewing a public profile manually rarely breaks any platform rule. Running an automated script to harvest thousands of profiles usually does. Know which side of that line your technique sits on before you use it.

Your persona rules from Week 3 are now live▶

This is the first week you may actually use the investigation persona you built. Everything from Week 3 applies in full: no reused photos, no personal details, correct browser container, VPN active. If you have not finished setting that up properly, do it before starting Tab 6.

Data protection applies even to public information▶

Under the NDPA 2023, information about an identifiable person is personal data whether or not it was posted publicly. Collecting it for a specific, legitimate investigative purpose and handling it carefully, as covered in Week 1, is generally sound practice. Collecting it out of curiosity, or holding onto it longer than the case requires, is not.

One firm line. Never use a persona to send a friend request, direct message, or any form of contact to a real subject without explicit authorisation from your mentor and a clear investigative justification. Passive observation of public content is very different from active engagement, and active engagement carries far more legal and ethical weight. This week's practice exercise is entirely passive observation of a fictional subject.

Why the passive versus active line matters so much

Sending a message, even an innocent seeming one, to a real subject changes the entire legal and ethical character of what you are doing. Passive observation of something a person chose to post publicly is one thing. Actively reaching out under a false identity to elicit information a person would not otherwise share is a form of deception that courts, platforms, and regulators treat very differently, and that this program treats very differently too. It is also simply less reliable evidence, a subject who suspects they are being approached by an investigator, even unknowingly, may behave differently than they would under ordinary observation, contaminating the very findings you were trying to gather.

Different platform, different technique

The six platform playbook

Every platform reveals different things, in different ways, to people who know where to look. Click each card.

It helps to think of each platform as having its own default privacy posture, baked in by how the product was designed rather than by anything an individual user chose. A platform built around professional identity, like LinkedIn, defaults toward openness because visibility is the entire point of the product. A platform built around private messaging, like Telegram, defaults toward closure, and what leaks through publicly is often unintentional. Knowing a platform's default posture tells you, before you even open it, roughly how much you should expect to find versus how much extra effort it will take to find anything at all.

LinkedIn

Professional
  • Employment history and gaps often reveal more than the stated job title itself
  • Shared connections can link a subject to a wider network worth mapping
  • Endorsements and recommendations sometimes name people not mentioned anywhere else
  • A profile created very recently, with few connections, for someone claiming a senior role, is itself a finding

X (Twitter)

Real time
  • Advanced search operators narrow by date, account, and even replies to a specific post
  • A thread's full history often survives even if the original account later deletes individual posts, through quote posts and replies
  • Who an account follows is frequently more revealing than who follows it back
  • Posting times across weeks can hint at a real timezone, regardless of a claimed location

Instagram

Visual
  • Geotags on photos, when present, can place a subject at a specific location and date
  • Being tagged in other people's photos reveals a social circle the subject does not control
  • Story highlights often persist long after the original 24 hour story expired
  • Backgrounds, reflections, and shadows in photos can be analysed the same way you practised in Week 1

Facebook

Personal
  • The About section, when public, often lists a work history that can be cross checked elsewhere
  • Mutual friends, when visible, can connect a subject to other people already of interest
  • Public group memberships reveal interests and affiliations a subject may not advertise elsewhere
  • Older posts, even years old, frequently remain indexed and searchable long after they are forgotten

TikTok

Short form
  • Background audio, locations, and other accounts tagged in a video can all be pivoted on
  • Comment sections sometimes reveal a subject's real name or relationships through friends tagging them
  • A sound or hashtag trend timestamp can help date a video even without an explicit post date
  • Duet and stitch videos can reveal a subject's connections to other creators

Telegram

Different rules
  • Public channels and groups can often be previewed without joining, unlike most other platforms
  • Usernames are unique platform wide, making correlation with other platforms more reliable
  • Covered in full depth in Tab 5, since Telegram's structure genuinely differs from the rest
The pattern underneath all six. No single platform tells the whole story. A LinkedIn gap plus an Instagram geotag plus a Facebook mutual friend, none of them conclusive alone, can together build a picture strong enough to act on. This is exactly why Tab 6 asks you to check every platform rather than stopping at the first one that looks promising.
One handle, many platforms

Username and email correlation

Most people reuse the same username, or a close variant of it, across many platforms without thinking twice. Tools like Sherlock and WhatsMyName exploit exactly that habit, checking a single username against hundreds of sites in one pass.

Try a simulated correlation search

This mimics what Sherlock does from your own terminal. Type a username or use the one already filled in, then run the search.

Reading a result like this. Found on X, Instagram, and Telegram but nowhere else, especially nowhere with a longer history, such as an old forum or a gaming platform, often points to an identity built specifically and recently for one purpose. A username with a long tail across dozens of older, unrelated services usually belongs to someone with a genuinely long standing online presence. The shape of the results matters as much as the results themselves.

Email correlation

An email address can be checked too, without ever attempting to access the account itself. Two free, legitimate techniques:

TechniqueWhat it tells you
Gravatar lookupMany people unknowingly have a Gravatar image linked to their email from years ago, sometimes revealing an old photo or username tied to that address.
Have I Been PwnedA free, legitimate service that shows which known data breaches an email address has appeared in, without exposing the actual leaked password. This can reveal which older platforms an email was once registered on, another pivot point.
Where this stops. Checking whether an email appears in a breach database is legitimate and widely used in professional OSINT. Attempting to use any password found in a breach to log into an account, even out of curiosity, is unauthorised access and a serious offence under the Cybercrimes Act. The line between looking and touching matters enormously here.

Why people reuse usernames despite knowing better

Most people intellectually understand that reusing a username everywhere is not ideal, and do it anyway, because remembering a different identifier for every service they have ever signed up for is genuinely impractical without dedicated tooling most people never bother installing. This is not a character flaw investigators get to feel superior about, it is simply an extremely common human habit, and understanding why it happens is what makes correlation such a reliable technique. A username someone chose once, casually, for a forum account in 2015, often turns out to be the exact same handle they used to register a professional LinkedIn account a decade later, because changing habits is more effort than most people ever consciously decide to spend on it.

A platform with its own rulebook

Telegram OSINT

Telegram behaves differently from every platform in Tab 3. Public channels and groups can often be previewed from an ordinary web browser, without an account, without joining, and without the channel owner ever knowing you looked.

Part of why Telegram sits apart from the other five platforms is architectural. Telegram channels are built as one way broadcast tools, closer to a public bulletin board than a private conversation, and the platform deliberately makes that broadcast content viewable outside the app itself to encourage discovery and growth. Private one to one chats and most groups remain properly closed off, but a channel's own creators have generally chosen public visibility as the entire point of running it, which is exactly why the web preview technique below works at all.
